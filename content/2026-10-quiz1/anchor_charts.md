# Grade 5 Quiz 1 — Reading Anchor Charts

Original supplementary study guides, not transcriptions of the missing teacher handouts.
The first five charts match the study notice; the remaining six support the app’s reading questions.

## Author's Purpose — Quiz 1 focus

The author’s purpose is the main reason for writing. Read the whole text and ask what the author wants you to think, learn, feel, or do.

### At a glance

- **Persuade:** Convince you to agree or act. Look for an opinion supported by reasons and evidence.
- **Inform:** Teach facts about a topic. Look for explanations, facts, and examples.
- **Entertain:** Engage you with a story, poem, or amusing events. A story can also teach a lesson.
- **Explain:** Show how or why something happens. This is often a type of informing.

### How to use it

1. State what the text mostly does.
2. Find two details that support that purpose.
3. Choose the main purpose, even if the text does more than one thing.

**Watch out:** A fact can support a persuasive text. A funny sentence does not make the whole text entertainment.

### Worked examples

- Schools Should Fund Young Inventors tries to persuade: the author states that schools should fund inventors, then gives reasons and examples.
- Alexander Graham Bell mainly informs by describing his life and inventions. Benny and Larry mainly entertains through a humorous fishing story.

### Quick check

“Our school should plant a garden. Students could study insects, and the cafeteria could use the vegetables.” What is the main purpose?

**Answer:** To persuade. “Should” states an opinion; learning about insects and growing food are reasons to agree.

## Text Structure — Quiz 1 focus

Text structure is the way an author organizes information. A paragraph and the whole text may use different structures.

### At a glance

- **Description · topic → details:** Tells what something is like. Clues: for example, such as, includes.
- **Sequence · first → next → last:** Puts events or steps in order. Clues: before, next, later, finally, dates.
- **Cause and effect · why → result:** Explains why something happens and what happens because of it. Clues: because, so, as a result.
- **Compare and contrast · alike ↔ different:** Compares two subjects on the same feature. Clues: both, unlike, however, whereas.
- **Problem and solution · difficulty → response:** Presents a difficulty and an attempt to solve it. Clues: problem, to solve, a solution.

### How to use it

1. Identify the ideas being connected.
2. Explain their relationship in your own words.
3. Use clue words to confirm the structure.

**Watch out:** “Then” is a clue, not proof. Decide how the ideas relate, rather than choosing from one word.

### Worked examples

- Captain Morgan: losing fresh water is the problem; collecting rainwater is the solution.
- The Flying Bathtub: wind fills the sail as the tub speeds downhill (cause), and the front wheels lift (effect).

### Quick check

“The bridge washed away. To get students across the stream, the town built a temporary footbridge.” Which structure fits best?

**Answer:** Problem and solution: the lost bridge creates a crossing problem, and the temporary bridge addresses it.

## Figurative Language — Quiz 1 focus

Figurative language helps readers picture an idea or feel an emotion. Explain both the type of expression and what it means in context.

### At a glance

- **Simile:** Compares unlike things using like or as: “Her idea shone like a star.” It suggests brilliance.
- **Metaphor:** Describes one thing as another: “The classroom was a zoo.” It suggests noise and disorder.
- **Personification:** Gives human qualities or actions to something nonhuman: “The wind whispered.” It suggests a soft sound.
- **Hyperbole:** Exaggerates for effect: “I waited a million years.” It means the wait felt very long.
- **Idiom:** A familiar phrase with a meaning beyond its individual words: “a piece of cake” means easy.

### How to use it

1. Find the unusual expression.
2. Ask what it means in this scene.
3. Explain how it helps you imagine the action or understand a feeling.

**Watch out:** “I like science” is not a simile. “Like” must make a comparison, and literal comparisons are not always figurative.

### Worked examples

- “He danced around the barn like a puppet with tangled strings” is a simile in The Flying Bathtub. It makes Rafael’s excited movements seem awkward and wild.
- “Mara’s stomach did a small flip” expresses nervousness; her stomach does not literally turn over.

### Quick check

“The old stairs complained under my feet.” What technique is used, and what does it mean?

**Answer:** Personification. Stairs cannot complain like a person; the expression helps us imagine their creaking sound.

## Central Idea — Quiz 1 focus

The central idea is the most important point the author makes about a topic. Supporting details explain or develop that point.

### At a glance

- **Topic · a few words:** What the text is about: “school invention projects.”
- **Central idea · a complete sentence:** What the author says about it: “Schools should fund student inventions because the projects benefit students.”
- **Supporting details · proof and explanation:** Reasons and examples: planning and testing develop skills; small budgets can make projects possible.

### How to use it

1. Name the topic.
2. Look for an idea that connects details across the text.
3. Write a full sentence and check that several important details support it.

**Watch out:** A central idea must fit the whole section being asked about. One interesting fact is usually too narrow.

### Worked examples

- In Schools Should Fund Young Inventors, the central claim is that schools should fund student inventions. The reasons about skills, costs, and valuing ideas support it.
- For a paragraph about building, testing, and improving a prototype, “Inventors improve ideas through repeated testing” covers more than “One model broke.”

### Quick check

“Bees carry pollen between flowers. This helps many plants make seeds. Farmers depend on bees to pollinate many crops.” State the central idea.

**Answer:** Bees help plants reproduce by moving pollen. The details about seeds and crops both support that idea.

## Point of View — Quiz 1 focus

Point of view identifies who tells a story and whose thoughts the narrator reveals. The narrator and the author are not necessarily the same person.

### At a glance

- **First person · inside the story:** A character narrates using I, me, my, we, or our. We learn what that narrator experiences and thinks.
- **Third-person limited · one mind:** An outside narrator uses names, he, she, or they and reveals one character’s thoughts or feelings.
- **Third-person omniscient · more than one mind:** An outside narrator can reveal multiple characters’ thoughts and information beyond any one character’s knowledge.
- **Third-person objective · actions only:** An outside narrator reports observable actions and dialogue without directly revealing inner thoughts.

### How to use it

1. Read the narration outside quotation marks.
2. Decide whether the narrator is a character.
3. For third person, track whose inner thoughts are actually revealed.

**Watch out:** “I” inside dialogue does not make the narration first person. Seeing several characters does not make a narrator omniscient.

### Worked examples

- The Flying Bathtub uses third-person limited narration centered on Mara. We learn her worries and feelings; Rafael explains his thinking through dialogue.
- “I hid my drawing” is first person. “Mia feared losing; Ben secretly hoped she would win” reveals two minds and is omniscient.

### Quick check

“Leo smiled. ‘I can do it,’ he said. Inside, he worried that his hands would shake.” What point of view does this excerpt show?

**Answer:** Third-person limited. The narrator uses Leo/he outside dialogue and reveals only Leo’s private worry.

## Theme — Supporting skill

A theme is a message about life that a story develops. Readers infer it from the characters, their choices, and the outcome.

### At a glance

- **Topic:** A broad subject such as friendship, courage, or persistence.
- **Theme:** A full message: “Friends can enjoy time together even when their plans fail.”
- **Evidence:** Actions and outcomes that support the message, rather than just a matching word.

### How to use it

1. Identify the character’s challenge.
2. Notice the response, change, and outcome.
3. Write a message that applies beyond these characters, then support it with details.

**Watch out:** A theme is not a plot summary. More than one theme can fit if the story supports it.

### Worked examples

- Benny and Larry supports the theme that enjoying friendship can matter more than achieving a goal: their fishing trip goes wrong, but they still have fun.
- The Flying Bathtub supports persistence despite ridicule: Rafael celebrates a brief flight and begins planning improvements.

### Quick check

A girl loses a race, keeps training, and improves her time at the next meet. Which is a stronger theme: “racing” or “Steady effort helps us improve”? Why?

**Answer:** “Steady effort helps us improve” is a full message supported by her training and better time. “Racing” is only a topic.

## Character — Supporting skill

Use a character’s actions, words, thoughts, and choices to infer traits and explain change.

### At a glance

- **Trait · usual quality:** Persistent, generous, or cautious describes a pattern of behavior.
- **Feeling · current emotion:** Worried, relieved, or excited describes an emotion at a particular moment.
- **Motivation · reason for acting:** What a character wants, needs, fears, or values.
- **Change · beginning → ending:** How an experience changes a character’s feelings, actions, or understanding.

### How to use it

1. Choose a trait, feeling, or motivation that answers the question.
2. Find an action, thought, or line of dialogue as evidence.
3. Explain how that evidence supports your inference.

**Watch out:** A character can feel afraid and still act bravely. Do not confuse a temporary feeling with a lasting trait.

### Worked examples

- Rafael is persistent: after a short flight and a splash, he starts sketching an additional propeller.
- Mara worries about people laughing at Rafael, yet she picks up a wrench to help. Her action suggests loyalty to family.

### Quick check

“Even after three towers fell, Ana rebuilt her model and tested a wider base.” Name a trait and give evidence.

**Answer:** Ana is persistent. She keeps rebuilding after three failures and tries a new base instead of giving up.

## Ideas and Support — Supporting skill

A writer develops an idea with reasons, facts, examples, or explanations. In an opinion text, distinguish the claim from its support.

### At a glance

- **Claim · what I want you to accept:** “Schools should support young inventors.”
- **Reason · why it makes sense:** “Invention projects teach problem-solving.”
- **Evidence · specific support:** A described project, observation, example, or relevant study can support the reason.
- **Explanation · the connection:** Tell how that evidence makes the reason or claim stronger.

### How to use it

1. Identify the author’s idea or claim.
2. Separate the reasons from the specific evidence.
3. Check whether each detail actually supports that idea.

**Watch out:** A strong-sounding opinion is not evidence. Specific details must be relevant; numbers alone do not prove a claim.

### Worked examples

- The student-inventor passage argues that small amounts of money can help. Its example of a $15 project budget supports that reason within the practice text.
- The same passage addresses an opposing view about other school expenses, then suggests bake sales and local support. This is a counterargument and response.

### Quick check

Claim: “Our library needs more opening hours.” Which supports it: “I like the wall color” or “Many students arrive after it has closed”? Explain.

**Answer:** The second detail supports longer hours because it shows that students cannot currently use the library when they arrive.

## Text Evidence — Supporting skill

Text evidence is a specific detail from the passage that supports an answer. You can quote exactly or paraphrase accurately.

### At a glance

- **Answer:** Make a clear statement that answers the question.
- **Evidence:** Choose a relevant action, fact, or quotation. Name the paragraph when helpful.
- **Explain:** Show how the detail supports your answer: “This shows … because …”.

### How to use it

1. Underline the important words in the question.
2. Return to the passage and choose the strongest matching detail.
3. Connect the evidence to your answer in your own words.

**Watch out:** Copying a long paragraph is not enough. Avoid adding facts the passage never states or changing words inside quotation marks.

### Worked examples

- Answer: Rafael does not give up. Evidence: he has already sketched a second propeller at the end of The Flying Bathtub. Explanation: he is planning another attempt after the crash.
- To show Mara’s worry, use her thoughts or physical reaction. A description of the harbor would not support that answer.

### Quick check

Question: Is Noor helpful? Text: “Noor put down her game and carried the heavy bags for her neighbor.” Write an answer with evidence.

**Answer:** Noor is helpful because she stops playing to carry her neighbor’s heavy bags. Giving up her own activity to assist someone supports the trait.

## Vocabulary in Context — Supporting skill

Use nearby words, sentences, and events to infer the meaning of an unfamiliar word. Then test that meaning in the sentence.

### At a glance

- **Definition or restatement:** “The device was portable, or easy to carry.” The explanation gives the meaning.
- **Example:** “She packed provisions: bread, fruit, and drinking water.” The examples suggest supplies.
- **Contrast:** “Unlike the noisy hall, the room was tranquil.” The contrast suggests calm or quiet.
- **Situation:** A character shivers, wraps up, and moves near a heater. These actions can help explain a word describing the cold.

### How to use it

1. Read before and after the unfamiliar word.
2. Find a clue and suggest a meaning that fits the context.
3. Substitute your meaning and reread; check word parts if useful.

**Watch out:** Choose the meaning used in this passage. A word may have several dictionary meanings.

### Worked examples

- In The Flying Bathtub, Rafael wears a fishing hat in the bathtub and a bathing cap to fish. These unusual habits help explain eccentric as unconventional or unusual.
- “The fragile model broke when it fell.” Breaking helps suggest that fragile means easily damaged.

### Quick check

“Water was scarce; the hikers had only one small bottle left for the whole group.” What does scarce mean, and what is the clue?

**Answer:** Scarce means in short supply. One small bottle for the whole group shows that there is very little water available.

## Compare and Contrast — Supporting skill

Comparing identifies similarities; contrasting identifies differences. Use the same feature to compare characters, events, ideas, or two texts.

### At a glance

- **Choose a feature:** Compare purposes, challenges, responses, outcomes, or a shared topic.
- **Find similarities:** Use both, similarly, or alike. Support the similarity with details about both subjects.
- **Find differences:** Use unlike, however, or whereas. Explain how the subjects differ on the same feature.
- **Draw a conclusion:** Explain what the comparison helps you understand.

### How to use it

1. Name the two subjects and the feature you will compare.
2. Gather one relevant detail for each subject.
3. Write a similarity or difference that uses both details.

**Watch out:** Do not compare one character’s age with another’s personality. Keep the comparison on the same feature.

### Worked examples

- Practice example: both Ana and Ben build models. Ana tests after each change, whereas Ben tests only the finished model. The shared feature is their testing method.
- Two texts about inventors may have different purposes: one gives facts about an inventor, while another argues that inventors deserve funding.

### Quick check

“Lina practiced alone every morning. Kai practiced with a team each evening.” Give one similarity and one difference.

**Answer:** Both practice regularly. Lina practices alone in the morning, whereas Kai practices with a team in the evening.
